# Biblical peoples, Black identity, and medieval representations

Research review — 3 October 2026, America/Los_Angeles.

**There is substantial evidence for Black African people in the biblical world, Black Jewish communities, and African interpretations and representations of biblical history. The sources examined do not establish that all Israelites were Black, or that Alexander the Great and Seleucus were Black Edomites.** Those are separate claims requiring separate evidence. This review identifies useful supporting material, its limits, and contrary evidence rather than treating every reference to dark skin as the same kind of statement.

This is research for the People and Timeline work, not published person data or a portrait approval. “Black,” “Ethiopian,” “Cushite,” “Moor,” geographic origin, and ancestry are not interchangeable across centuries. Ancient descriptions of skin color can be meaningful without mapping precisely onto a present-day racial identity.

## Findings by claim

| Claim | Best evidence examined | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Black African people participated in the biblical world | Jeremiah's Ebed-melech; Numbers 12:1; Goldenberg, chapters 1–2; *Who's Who*, Cush/Ebed-melech entries | Supported for particular people and populations. The identification of Moses's wife and some personal names remains debated. |
| Some members of Israelite society had African connections or ancestry | Cush/Cushi names; Phinehas's Egyptian name; Goldenberg, pp. 38–40 | Substantial research leads, with differing explanations of individual names. An etymology alone does not establish a person's ancestry or complexion. |
| All ancient Israelites had one Black racial identity | Biblical color passages and later descriptions of Jews | Not established. Individual descriptions, poetry, regional communities, and modern racial classifications need to be kept distinct. |
| Medieval sources describe Black Jews | Benjamin of Tudela, *Itinerary*, Chulah, pp. 140–141 in Asher's translation | Explicitly supported as a medieval report about a particular community in India. It is not a census of ancient Israel or necessarily an eyewitness account. |
| African biblical traditions existed in the Middle Ages | *Kebra Nagast*, Ethiopian Solomon–Sheba–Menyelek narrative | Supported as a medieval Ethiopian tradition of biblical ancestry and kingship; not independent contemporary evidence for Solomon's appearance. |
| Christian art represented holy figures as Black | Walters Museum catalogue, Saint Maurice; specialist bibliography on the Black Magus | Supported for identified artworks and periods. The inspected Maurice painting is about 1515–1520, so it belongs to the Renaissance, not a casually extended “Middle Ages.” |
| Alexander the Great was a Black Edomite | 1 Maccabees 1:1; Plutarch, *Alexander* 4; *Who's Who*, p. 405 | No reliable supporting identification found. Plutarch's description of a fair complexion is contrary evidence, although written centuries afterward. |
| Seleucus was a Black Edomite | Appian, *Syrian Wars* 52–57; *Who's Who*, pp. 439–440 | No supporting identification found. Distinguish Seleucus I Nicator from Seleucus IV Philopator, the ruler in 2 Maccabees 3. |
| These facts were deliberately hidden worldwide | Publication and transmission history of the sources examined | Not established by this research. Relevant material is openly discussed in reference books, university-press scholarship, museum publications, and historical texts. |

## 2. Academic research that directly addresses African people in biblical society

**David M. Goldenberg, *The Curse of Ham: Race and Slavery in Early Judaism, Christianity, and Islam* (Princeton University Press, 2003).** [Library catalogue record](https://archive.org/details/curseofhamracesl0000gold); [accessible scanned text examined](https://archive.org/details/goldenberg-curse-of-ham).

Relevant passages were read, not inferred from the title. **Pages 17–40** examine Kush's geography and Africans in biblical society. **Page 39** discusses Phinehas's Egyptian name and competing interpretations of Cush/Cushi names. **Pages 93–94** distinguish physiological color changes from inherited complexion. **Page 95**, with notes on **pp. 281–282**, discusses an intermediate/light-brown complexion in Mishnah *Negaim* 2:1 and honey-colored/dark-colored descriptions in Egyptian papyri.

These are person-, place-, and period-specific observations. They do not justify assigning one modern racial identity to every Israelite. A responsible app note should carry those limits with its citation. The book's title is not an endorsement of the “curse of Ham” interpretation.

Two specific scholarly follow-ups cited by Goldenberg are **Shaye J. D. Cohen, *The Beginnings of Jewishness* (1999), pp. 29–30**, and **I. F. Fikhman, “The Physical Appearance of Egyptian Jews according to the Greek Papyri,” *Scripta Classica Israelica* 18 (1999)**. Their references were checked in Goldenberg's notes; their original texts were not independently retrieved in this pass.

## 3. Scripture: direct evidence and the context of color language

All scripture quotations below come directly from the repository's KJV files, not the different Bible translation used inside *Who's Who*. The pinned corpus is [DevSecObie/cyberjudah at ee09d540](https://github.com/DevSecObie/cyberjudah/tree/ee09d540382dfc8c64be363a95af1b5017fe2737/data/bible).

| Passage | What it provides | Limit of the inference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Jeremiah 38:7; 39:16 | Explicitly calls Ebed-melech “the Ethiopian” and places him in Judah's royal setting and Jeremiah's rescue narrative | Strong specific evidence; it does not describe every inhabitant of Judah. |
| Numbers 12:1 | “the Ethiopian woman whom he had married” | An important African connection in Moses's story. The woman's identity and the geographic interpretation receive differing scholarly treatments. |
| Song of Solomon 1:5–6 | “I am black, but comely” and the speaker's explanation about the sun and vineyard work | Explicit dark complexion in the poem. The speaker is a woman addressing the daughters of Jerusalem; this is not Solomon describing himself or a declaration about all Israelites. |
| Jeremiah 13:23 | Refers to the Ethiopian's skin as something he cannot change | A particular comparison involving complexion, not an equation of all Israelites with Ethiopians. |
| Amos 9:7 | Compares Israel with the children of the Ethiopians “unto me” and immediately mentions several peoples' migrations | A theological comparison. A claim about identical complexion or genealogy needs an additional argument; the verse does not state one. |
| Lamentations 4:7–9; 5:10 | Contrasts former condition with famine and withering; 5:10 explicitly says “because of the terrible famine” | The context makes suffering indispensable to interpretation. Quoting only “blacker than a coal” removes that context. |
| Job 30:30 | Describes skin becoming black alongside bodily suffering | Not, by itself, an ancestral identification. |
| Revelation 1:14–15 | White hair compared with wool and snow; feet compared with fine brass in a furnace | Visionary imagery. The hair comparison explicitly includes color; it does not explicitly specify tightly curled hair. A theological depiction can be discussed as interpretation without presenting it as an eyewitness portrait. |

The accompanying `scripture-evidence.json` preserves the complete quoted verses exactly, including the corpus's punctuation. No racial label has been inserted into those verses or into a person's record.

## 4. A medieval account of Black Jews

**Benjamin of Tudela, *The Itinerary*, a twelfth-century travel account; A. Asher's 1840 English translation, pp. 138–141, especially 140–141.** [Scanned volume](https://archive.org/details/itineraryrabbib00benjgoog).

In the account of **Chulah**, in the Indian Ocean portion of the itinerary, the Jewish community is described as people:

> “who are of black colour as well as the other inhabitants.”

The surrounding passage describes observance of Jewish law and knowledge of Jewish texts. This directly supports a medieval report of a dark-skinned Jewish community. It does not identify every Jewish community in the world as Black, and India should not be silently replaced with Africa. The account includes distant reports as well as travel narrative; this passage should be attributed to Benjamin's account without claiming that he personally inspected the community.

The 1901–1906 *Jewish Encyclopedia*, “India,” vol. VI, p. 581, also discusses Benjamin's report. Its account and older translations differ over details such as population counts, so the proposed app wording should not depend on an unverified head count. The encyclopedia's old racial terminology and theories are historical evidence, not automatically present-day scholarly conclusions.

## 5. Medieval Ethiopian biblical tradition and later European art

**The *Kebra Nagast*, translated by E. A. Wallis Budge as *The Queen of Sheba & Her Only Son Menyelek* (1922).** [Public-domain edition](https://archive.org/details/queenofshebahero00budgrich).

The narrative connects Ethiopian kingship to Solomon, the Queen of Sheba, their son Menyelek, and the transfer of the Ark. Budge's introduction, pp. xxx–xxxi, discusses medieval compilation and competing readings of its colophon, including dates in the fourteenth century. This is important evidence for Ethiopian biblical reception and royal identity. The work's medieval form is many centuries later than the biblical setting; the genealogy it narrates should be identified as the tradition's claim, not treated as an independently verified portrait or pedigree of Solomon.

**Joaneath Spicer, ed., *Revealing the African Presence in Renaissance Europe* (Walters Art Museum, 2012).** [Catalogue record](https://archive.org/details/revealingafrican0000unse); [accessible catalogue examined](https://archive.org/details/revealing-the-african-presence-in-renaissance-europe).

The catalogue provides scholarship about actual Africans in Europe and about representation. Its discussion of **Saint Maurice** and catalogue entry **26**, *St. Maurice and the Theban Legion*, South German master, ca. 1515–1520, document a Black holy figure in European religious art. The discussion also notes variation in Maurice's depiction between regions. This is evidence about the artwork and cult, not a contemporary image of the late-antique man represented.

The catalogue's notes supply more specialized research: **Jean Devisse, “A Sanctified Black: Maurice,” in *The Image of the Black in Western Art*, II.1 (2010 edition), pp. 150–194**, and **Gude Suckale-Redlefsen and Robert Suckale, *The Black Saint Maurice* (1987)**. These original texts were not fully retrieved here. The often-cited medieval Maurice at Magdeburg and the development of the Black Magus deserve object-specific follow-up before choosing art for the app.

For **Balthazar/the Black Magus**, the catalogue record for **Paul H. D. Kaplan, *The Rise of the Black Magus in Western Art* (1985)** was verified, but its full text is access-restricted: [record](https://archive.org/details/riseofblackmagus0000kapl). I am not presenting an uninspected passage or artwork as verified evidence. A depiction centuries after the biblical episode establishes a history of representation; its ability to establish the represented person's actual appearance requires a separate argument.

## 6. Alexander: examine the ancient account directly

**Plutarch, *Life of Alexander* 4.1–3, Bernadotte Perrin translation, *Lives* VII, pp. 231–233.** [Scanned p. 231, checked visually](https://archive.org/details/L099PlutarchParallelLivesVIIDemosthenesCiceroAlexanderJuliusCaesar/page/n246/mode/1up).

Plutarch says that Apelles's painted Alexander was too dark and swarthy, then describes Alexander as:

> “of a fair colour, as they say”

The next page describes ruddiness, especially on his breast and face. This is genuine contrary evidence to the proposed complexion claim. Plutarch wrote centuries after Alexander, and his report is not an exact color measurement; “fair” also should not be converted automatically into a modern political racial identity. Nevertheless, an honest source review must include the passage.

1 Maccabees 1:1 calls him **“Alexander son of Philip, the Macedonian”**. That establishes the text's identification, not a skin tone. Neither this passage nor the examined *Who's Who* entry supplies Edomite ancestry. A later Ethiopian, Persian, or European portrayal of Alexander would need to be identified as that culture's later portrayal before using it.

## 7. Seleucus, Edom, and the need to distinguish literal ancestry from interpretation

**Appian, *Roman History*, book XI, *Syrian Wars* 52–57; Horace White translation (1899), vol. I, pp. 312–316.** [Scanned edition](https://archive.org/details/romanhistoryofap01appi).

Appian places Seleucus I's rise in the succession to Alexander and the Macedonian commanders' division of power. Section 57 says Seleucus named cities Antioch after his father and Laodicea after his mother. It contains no identification of him as Black or Edomite. Absence in one source does not establish his exact complexion; it does mean this source cannot responsibly be cited as supporting the proposed claim.

For **Seleucus IV**, use the separate *Who's Who* entry and 2 Maccabees 3–4. Do not merge the ruler in that narrative with the dynasty's founder. No examined source establishes the requested ancestry/complexion for either ruler.

**“Edom,” *The Jewish Encyclopedia*, vol. V, pp. 40–41**, distinguishes ancient Edom/Idumea from the later use of Edom for Rome and Christianity. [P. 41, scan leaf 72](https://archive.org/details/cu31924091768220/page/n72/mode/1up). It records the symbolic use by rabbinic authors and medieval poets. That is a documented interpretive tradition; the article does not give a historical genealogy making Macedonian rulers descendants of Esau.

Genesis 36:1 identifies Esau with Edom. Genesis 25:25 and 25:30 contain the red/hairy description and red-pottage naming narrative. These texts do not name Alexander or Seleucus. Connections made through later theological interpretation should be attributed to that interpretation, rather than converted into an independently established ethnographic fact.

## 8. A historical work explicitly arguing for Black biblical ancestry

**R. B. Lewis, *Light and Truth* (Boston, 1844).** Already in the app's Library. [Public-domain scan](https://archive.org/details/lightandtruthcoll00lewirich).

This is worth retaining in the research because it actually advances related claims. On **p. 15**, after a genealogy of Shem's descendants, Lewis calls them black and attributes the statement to an unnamed Greek historian. On **pp. 246–249**, he uses accounts of Black Jews in India in his argument about Israel's dispersal. These are concrete passages to examine, not merely a book title that sounds supportive.

Their evidentiary status must be accurate: Lewis is a nineteenth-century author making a historical argument, not a witness to ancient Israel. The unnamed historian on p. 15 needs to be traced before treating the assertion as corroborated ancient testimony. His classification also differs from other interpretations: **p. 24** calls Esau's descendants “the Red People” and equates Edomites with Indians, while **p. 369** calls Alexander Egyptian. The book therefore cannot be treated as a consistent, independent proof of the specific Black-Edomite-Macedonian claim.

Use it as **“R. B. Lewis argued in 1844…”**, with exact references. Test each assertion against its underlying source. Its age and public-domain status do not themselves establish accuracy, just as a newer publication's prestige does not remove the need to inspect its evidence.

## Application to the approved app work

Keep the original Bible Strong Timeline layout and controls. Research does not require a new navigation system.

For People, retain verse-based identity facts and person-specific citations. Ebed-melech's designation, Phinehas's name history, a medieval report about a Jewish community, and a painting's iconography are different facts and should be labelled accordingly. For Alexander, Seleucus, and the sample Apocrypha people, this research has not verified a blanket Black/Edomite descriptor; do not insert one as settled biography.

Historical and artistic claims can have dated, attributed source notes. Teaching excerpts belong under the existing **From the classes / Quotes and sources** pattern, verbatim and linked to a verified moment. This research pass did not verify new class quotations and should not manufacture them to close a gap in historical evidence. New portraits remain subject to the owner's existing approval rule.
